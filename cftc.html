Overview

I designed a project tracker that connects one of Sudan’s largest nonprofits with donors around the world.

Sadagaat has reached 4 million+ people in the last five years through water, food, health, and education projects. Each month, its Sudan branch emailed a spreadsheet of open projects to international partners, who claimed them by email or WhatsApp. With no shared source of truth, multiple partners often claimed the same project.

As a design intern at Code for the Community, I designed the dashboard and project upload flow that replace this process with one live list where each project can only be claimed once.

Some of my accomplishments include:

Ended duplicate claims

Designed a shared, live project list where each project can only be claimed once, replacing monthly spreadsheets and WhatsApp threads.

Designed for real constraints

Interviewed Sadagaat leadership and found staff have just 10–15 minutes to review projects, so I made status visible at a glance and kept their familiar spreadsheet workflow.

Set the team’s direction

My dashboard set the visual direction for the project. I worked with the engineers and PM to make sure the designs could be built.

Solution

One shared, live list where a project can only be claimed once.

I designed the project tracking dashboard and new project workflow, allowing Sadagaat and its international entities to view one shared, live list of open projects and for Sadagaat Sudan to upload new projects from Excel sheets.

The workflow

  1. One list for every officeAll open projects across each sector and state in one place with search, filter, and sort features.
  2. Preview and save as draftBefore publishing, users can preview how their uploaded Excel sheet will appear, or save it as a draft to continue working on later.
  3. Upload history and needs your actionUpload history keeps a record of past uploads, and the Needs your action feature surfaces the projects waiting on you.

Research

I assessed user pain points from speaking with Sadagaat’s leadership in a series of user interviews.

We synthesized these findings into our affinity diagram, focusing on translating the insights we learned into tangible actions we could take in our designs.

Competitive analysis

Using Claude to find sources of inspiration.

Before designing, I used Claude to find websites and other sources of inspiration to analyze. For each one, I noted what it did right and what we could bring into the tracker.

From water accessibility nonprofits to Shopify and the US government, we were able to explore features and design choices to consider implementing. We learned that a tabbed dashboard with expandable rows would allow users to easily sort through dozens of projects, while also concealing detailed information that wouldn’t overwhelm the user at a first glance.

Key insights

With this newfound knowledge, 3 findings shaped our designs.

  1. 01
    Make status unmistakable

    A clear status tells an office at a glance whether a project can be claimed, so staff spend their limited time making decisions instead of decoding the list.

  2. 02
    Stick to what’s familiar

    Making sure we stay consistent with Sadagaat’s current language and workflow ensures users can migrate to our designs with little friction.

  3. 03
    Keep it brief and intuitive

    Staff only have 10-15 minutes to scan through a long list of projects, meaning our interface has to be easily navigable.

Iterations

When putting my designs together, I prioritized cleanliness and ease of navigation.

Expandable rows helped centralize the important information needed regarding a project.

The project stage diagram helps users visualize what part of the process the project is in.

Giving a preview of the project tables allows new users who aren’t as familiar with the app to look over their uploads and correct any mistakes that were made. The draft feature addresses Sadagaat’s issues of constantly changing project statuses.

Viewing upload history helps keep track of past uploads, tracing back project histories when needed.

Final designs

Dashboard, upload table and upload history.

I designed two parts of that experience: how projects are browsed and viewed, and how new project tables are uploaded and tracked over time.

Reflection

Some key takeaways and next steps:

  1. 01
    AI-native designing

    For this project, I designed exclusively in Claude Design after taking advice from my industry mentors about the importance of adopting AI tools as opposed to designing purely from scratch. I learned how to spot hallmarks of AI design, which helped me un-AI-slopify Claude’s initial output.

  2. 02
    Communicating effectively with stakeholders

    I learned firsthand how important it is to fully brief your interviewees with an overview of user interviewing before actually conducting them. Admittedly, I didn’t do this for one of our interviews, and the interview itself didn’t go the way I’d hoped it would because of it!

  3. 03
    Working alongside other designers

    This was my first project where I wasn’t the only designer on the team. This made me learn how to appropriately communicate and split up work to ensure both of our voices were heard.

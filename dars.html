DARS Redesign

Making UCLA’s degree audit clear enough to trust.

Timeline

Role
UI/UX designer, UX researcher
Context

Overview

UCLA’s degree audit is hard to read, and harder to trust.

UCLA’s Degree Audit Report System (DARS) presents students’ degree progress as one long, cluttered page. Fulfilled and unfulfilled requirements are listed sequentially, interrupted by long, unformatted lists of courses that could satisfy each gap. This density leaves little visual hierarchy for students to navigate, making the interface intimidating for first-time users. This project set out to identify the root causes of DARS’s usability issues and redesign the interface so key information is clear and impossible to overlook.

Solution

From one long list to a dashboard students can read at a glance.

I redesigned DARS around what students actually look for first, and made the next step, finding a class that fills a gap, one click away.

Previous experience

  1. One long, cluttered pageFulfilled and unfulfilled requirements are listed sequentially with little visual hierarchy.
  2. Course lists in the wayLong, unformatted lists of eligible courses interrupt each requirement.
  3. No connection to planningStudents flip back and forth between their audit and the class planner.

Redesigned experience

  1. A bento dashboardGPA, completed units, and incomplete requirements are visible at a glance.
  2. Class search by requirementFilter unfulfilled requirements to find eligible classes and add them to the planner.
  3. An AI assistantQuick, clarifying questions answered in a familiar chat interface.

Research

Students avoid DARS until they have no choice.

To understand the scope of this problem, we conducted user interviews with UCLA students to learn how they actually navigate DARS and where it breaks down for them. These conversations confirmed that the consequences go beyond frustration: some students have missed critical information buried in the audit, leading them to enroll in classes they didn’t actually need. Here are some of the key insights we found:

Unpleasant to use

Participants described the current interface as annoying, confusing, and overwhelming.

Poorly displays graduation progress

Despite all of the necessary information being displayed, users still feel unconfident and make mistakes planning their next steps.

New students avoid using DARS

Students reported that they often avoided using DARS in their 1st and 2nd years, only using it in their later years out of necessity.

No integration with other tools

No integration with other highly used tools, like UCLA’s class planner, forces students to repeatedly look back-and-forth when planning classes.

After processing the data from our user interviews, my team and I created an affinity diagram, user persona, root cause analysis, and finally, a list of requirements our final product would need to have in order to provide a better user experience for UCLA students.

Affinity diagramBy defining our user’s needs, this affinity diagram aided us in centering the core issues facing our users and ideating potential design changes we could implement.

Root cause analysisOur root cause analysis carefully outlines the specific processes our users go through when using DARS, making it easier for us to find the actual source of their issues with the current design rather than just fixing surface-level problems.

Requirements listLastly, the initial requirements list allowed me to prioritize certain features that will be included in our DARS redesign rather than going in blind.

How might we make a student’s graduation progress clear enough to trust, so they check it early instead of avoiding it?

Creating a user persona made it easier to consolidate our findings from our user interviews and practice empathy, one of the most important skills in user design and research.

Iterations

Testing showed we were close, but not there yet.

After collecting our findings and gaining a better understanding of the problem, we moved into creating our design. Given the time constraints of the class, designing had to be done quickly and efficiently to ensure we could collect a sizable sample in time. I was in charge of designing the dashboard and the AI chatbot interface, with the first iterations below.

Now that we had an interface to present (plus the class search design my groupmate created, not pictured), we conducted usability tests to see whether students found the newly redesigned interfaces to be intuitive, and what areas we needed to work on. Our key research highlights can be found below:

“The design is very pleasant to use. Definitely an upgrade from the current version of DARS. I really like how clean everything is.”

Along with these positive pieces of feedback, we received notice of several areas we could improve on. Namely, these included difficulty to understand the completed units chart (some students were unsure whether the “135/180” units included completed or in progress units, or were incomplete units) and the GPA chart (some students struggled with finding the GPA chart breakdown, which could be done by hovering over the “Detailed View” button in the “Current GPA” section).

Overall, though, it was nice to know we were heading in the right direction with our redesign of the website. Performing this research gave me the guidance I needed to continue our project independently following the end of this class.

Reflection

What I took away

Lived experience made the research sharper

This project was very fulfilling to work on, especially since I have shared the same frustrations our users have with the current DARS interface myself! Not only was I given the opportunity to learn about UX research methods, but I was able to apply my knowledge practically and practice the iterative process of designing and conducting research for improvement.

What I would do differently

If I were to repeat this project again, I would try to recruit incoming UCLA freshmen or users who are not familiar with the UCLA’s DARS feature to see if my design is intuitive to use for first-time users, especially since many of our interviewed students, who were limited to students taking the User Experience class, cited that their freshmen-selves were once intimidated by DARS.
